Every business handles sensitive data: contracts, customer data, banking
information, trade secrets. Data encryption is one of the essential links in the
cybersecurity of very small businesses and SMEs. By allowing only the holder of
the access keys to read, use or modify the data, it provides particularly
effective protection against hacking, theft and tampering.

This guide, written by Parsec, a data security specialist listed as a
[France Num](https://www.francenum.gouv.fr) Activator, explains what encryption
is, how it works and why it is now indispensable to any cybersecurity strategy,
whatever the size of your company.

## Why every small business and SME must protect its data

### Risks that affect businesses of every size

Cybersecurity is not a concern exclusive to large companies. According to the
2025 national barometer of cyber maturity among very small businesses and SMEs
published by [Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr](https://www.cybermalveillance.gouv.fr),
80% of them admit they are not sufficiently prepared for a cyberattack, and
nearly 6 in 10 admit they do not know how to assess the consequences of a
security incident.

Yet the stakes are vital. An analysis by Bessé / G.P. Goldstein of 48 cyber
incidents affecting unlisted French companies between 2017 and 2021 shows that
the risk of the company failing increases by about 50% in the 6 months following
the announcement of the incident, because it cannot absorb the costs of
recovery, the loss of business and any regulatory penalties.

### Sensitive data in every sector

All small businesses and SMEs handle sensitive personal data:

- customer files;
- employee data;
- banking information;
- contracts;
- etc.

Many companies also hold confidential documents covered by trade secrets or
intellectual property. Some sectors are particularly exposed:

- Car garages keep copies of vehicle registration certificates, documents with
  administrative value, often stored in a simple shared folder or a mailbox.
- Law firms and strategy consultants handle information covered by professional
  secrecy: ongoing disputes, financial structures, disposal transactions.
- Real estate professionals receive and forward payslips, bank statements and
  loan offers, all of which are personal data under the
  [GDPR](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/reglement-europeen-protection-donnees).
- Engineering firms and industrial subcontractors hold plans, pending patents
  and trade secrets that make up their competitive advantage.

In all these cases, a data leak can lead to financial penalties, a loss of
customer trust and legal liability for the company.

## What is data encryption?

Data encryption plays a crucial role in cybersecurity: it consists of
transforming readable data into a secret code that can only be decrypted with a
specific digital key. A mathematical algorithm (the encryption software) creates
an encryption scheme that, in theory, can only be broken with exceptional
computing power.

Imagine you slip a confidential document into a locked box before sending it by
post. Only the person who holds the key to that box can open it and read the
document; no one else, neither the postman nor the postal operator, can access
the content. Digital encryption works on exactly this principle: without the
matching key, it is impossible to recover the original content, even with a very
powerful computer.

The key is essential to access encrypted data. It can only be read or modified
after being decrypted.

In the event of theft or intrusion, encryption provides a robust barrier by
making the data unreadable without the appropriate key. The information remains
secure whether it is stored on computers, in transit, or during processing.

### From data encryption to cryptography

Cryptography refers to the set of mathematical techniques used to protect data.
It offers robust protection against data leaks, whether accidental or caused by
a malicious act. In particular, it ensures:

- **Data confidentiality**: encryption maintains data confidentiality by making
  it unreadable to anyone unauthorized. Even if someone manages to intercept the
  data, it is unusable without the appropriate decryption key.
- **Data integrity**: hackers do not just steal information, they can also alter
  data for fraudulent purposes. Through hash functions or message authentication
  codes, cryptography helps guarantee data integrity by detecting any
  unauthorized alteration or modification: any change made to encrypted data is
  detected during the decryption process, which reveals a breach of data
  integrity.
- **Data authentication**: cryptography is also used to verify the authenticity
  of data and of the sender. Electronic certificates and digital signatures make
  it possible to ensure that data comes from the expected source and has not
  been altered during transmission.

To learn more, read the [CNIL](https://www.cnil.fr) article (in French):
[Comprendre les grands principes de la cryptologie et du chiffrement](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/comprendre-les-grands-principes-de-la-cryptologie-et-du-chiffrement)
(Understanding the main principles of cryptology and encryption).

There are 2 types of encryption, depending on how the key is managed.

### Symmetric encryption: one key for everyone

In symmetric encryption, the same key is used both to encrypt and to decrypt the
document. It is the simplest system: you lock the box with a key, and your
recipient opens it with the same key. Compression software (a password-protected
ZIP archive, for example) works on this principle.

Simple to implement, it proves more limited for exchanging information. How can
you pass the key (the password) to the recipient without a third party being
able to intercept it? At the very least, different channels should be used: if
the encrypted document is sent by email, the key can be sent by SMS. This is the
fundamental problem of symmetric encryption whenever data must be shared with
other people.

Symmetric encryption is also used for disk encryption, which encrypts the entire
contents of a computer or a USB key; its strengths and limitations are detailed
below.

### Asymmetric encryption: one key to lock, another to open

Asymmetric encryption solves this problem with an ingenious idea: each user has
two keys that are mathematically linked but distinct.

- The sender's **public key** can be freely shared with everyone. It is used to
  encrypt a message for its owner.
- The **private key** is known only to its owner. Only it allows the recipient
  to decrypt the messages that others have sent them using their public key.

Imagine an open padlock (the public key) that you distribute freely: anyone can
take it, slip a document inside and close it. But you are the only one who holds
the key that opens this padlock (the private key). You can hand out as many open
padlocks as you like; no one will be able to open them in your place.

This is the principle behind the end-to-end encryption technologies used today
to secure communications and file sharing between several people.

## Why encrypt your company's data?

### A bulwark against ransomware attacks

Ransomware is a real threat to small businesses and SMEs. While it is not the
most frequent threat (it accounted for 8.1% of assistance requests to
[Cybermalveillance.gouv.fr](https://www.cybermalveillance.gouv.fr) in 2025), it
is one of the most formidable for an organization.

Malicious software enters the information system, often via a booby-trapped
email attachment, and encrypts all of the company's files, which then become
inaccessible. The attackers demand a ransom to give the data back. If a
company's data is centralized on a non-partitioned server, a single attack can
compromise everything at once.

### Protection against interception attacks

Companies increasingly use services hosted outside their premises, such as the
cloud or SaaS services, and fewer and fewer internal servers. As a result, their
data constantly travels over networks. If the data is not encrypted, it is
exposed to the risk of interception.

When data is encrypted, it is much harder for an attacker to intercept and
understand: even if they manage to obtain the encrypted data, they cannot read
it without the appropriate encryption key.

### Data sovereignty and regulatory compliance

The major digital service providers (cloud, online storage, office suites) are
mostly American. Their data, even when hosted in Europe, may be subject to US
legislation that allows US authorities to access it without necessarily
informing the European companies concerned. For a company handling data covered
by professional secrecy or falling under the European General Data Protection
Regulation
([GDPR](https://www.cnil.fr/fr/reglement-europeen-protection-donnees)), this
situation raises legitimate questions of compliance and legal liability.

Data protection regulations encourage or require data encryption, depending on
its level of sensitivity. Encryption helps meet the personal data protection
requirements set by the GDPR.

## Which encryption approach should you adopt?

### Understanding the three states of data

In its practical guide on encryption in the cloud, the
[CNIL](https://www.cnil.fr) distinguishes 3 states in which data can exist, and
points out that the protection measures to apply differ depending on the state:

1. **Data at rest** is stored persistently (on a hard drive or an SSD) without
   being actively used or transmitted.
2. **Data in transit** is data moving across a network: a sent email, a file
   transferred to online cloud storage, a message exchanged on an instant
   messaging service.
3. **Data in use** is data being viewed, updated or analyzed by a user, an
   application or a service.

In each of its states, data can benefit from encryption to improve the company's
level of security.

### Encryption applied to each state of data: a useful but limited approach

Disk encryption (data at rest), which encrypts the entire contents of a computer
or a USB key, ensures that if the hardware is stolen, no one can access the data
without the password. This is a useful protection that should always be enabled.

But as soon as the computer is turned on and connected, the data exchanged with
the outside (data in transit) is decrypted and becomes readable again by the
user, but also by any network or online service these files are then sent to.
Disk encryption therefore does not protect data once it leaves the device. To
encrypt data in transit, other encryption tools are needed, such as a VPN
solution that encrypts the data flows exchanged to make them unreadable.

Data sent to a remote server, cloud storage or a SaaS solution, such as a remote
ERP or CRM, and processed by these solutions (data in use), is also exposed if
it is stored in clear text (unencrypted), particularly in the event of a data
leak.

By not encrypting data in all of its states (at rest, in transit and in use),
you expose it to specific risks. End-to-end encryption addresses this issue by
ensuring continuous encryption throughout the use of the data.

### End-to-end encryption: reinforced protection

With end-to-end encryption, data is encrypted (and signed) before it even leaves
the sender's device, and is only decrypted (and verified) on arrival, on the
authorized recipient's device. In between, during transport, on the provider's
servers and in data centers, the data remains encrypted and unreadable to anyone
who does not hold the key.

This is the fundamental difference with a conventional cloud storage service,
which may encrypt data in transit and at rest, but keeps the decryption keys,
and can therefore, itself or through a third party compelling it, access the
data.

Some messaging applications, SMS and other communication services rely on
end-to-end encryption to protect messages against unauthorized access.

This approach makes it possible to implement a Zero Trust architecture, which
assumes that no person, device or application should be trusted by default,
whether inside or outside the company network.

This type of architecture should be favored to secure data as much as possible,
particularly in the context of information exchange and collaboration. It
prevents the company server that centralizes its data (whether hosted on its
premises or with a provider) from becoming a single point of vulnerability.

## The expert's advice

For a budget that is often equivalent to that of conventional solutions, it is
possible to move your data to a solution that includes end-to-end encryption and
thus benefits from the highest level of protection.

One point to anticipate: in an architecture where the provider never holds the
decryption keys, a password lost by an employee can make their files permanently
inaccessible, even to the provider itself. There is indeed no "back door" reset
procedure as on a conventional service. An internal procedure for backing up
access, such as setting up a shared password vault, is therefore recommended.
These tools include mechanisms for backing up personal keys, associated
reminders and cloning of one's device.

Another nuance to be aware of: end-to-end encryption protects the content of
files and messages, but not always the associated metadata (who communicates
with whom, at what time, the name or size of a file). Depending on the solution,
this information may remain visible to the provider. It is advisable to ask the
provider explicitly what is not covered by encryption before concluding that the
offer is completely watertight.

Finally, to make the choice of a provider more objective, the qualification or
certification issued by the French National Cybersecurity Agency
([ANSSI](https://cyber.gouv.fr)), a France Num partner, is a reliable benchmark:
it attests to an independent evaluation of the product's robustness and, for
qualification, to the provider's compliance with French regulatory requirements.
